# Empathy Extension: Critical Money Moments

## Status

This artifact reopens empathy after the first convergence exposed a narrower
question than the full job. It combines current-product evidence with
provisional behavioral hypotheses. The questions and protections below should
be tested with customers; they are not presented as completed user research.

## Central observation

Most people do not continuously “manage a budget.” They interact with their
finances at moments when an intention, expectation, or sense of safety becomes
vulnerable:

- desire is competing with a prior intention;
- a necessary expense has disrupted the plan;
- a familiar spending pattern suddenly feels questionable;
- a balance, transaction, or total looks wrong;
- income timing creates uncertainty;
- another household member needs an answer;
- the plan no longer matches real life.

The product opportunity is not to make every customer ask the same question.
It is to recognize the kind of moment, preserve the customer's own words, and
assemble the smallest trustworthy answer that resolves *their* question.

## The self-protection customers may want

People are unlikely to want “discipline” as a generic product behavior. They may
want one or more specific protections they deliberately choose:

### A pause before easy action

Create enough distance between impulse and purchase for the customer's existing
intention to become available again. This is especially useful when shopping,
delivery, gaming, or social apps compress desire and payment into seconds.

### Their own reason returned at the right moment

The most relevant protection may be a customer-owned sentence such as `I want
to keep $1,000 untouched`, `December gifts are already covered`, or `We are
protecting the family trip`. This should be recalled as evidence of their prior
choice, not as Kwilt moralizing.

### Consequences made concrete

Replace abstract limits with what the purchase changes: `Dining would have $45
left`, `this uses the rest of unassigned room`, or `the repair reserve remains
intact`. People may need visibility, not prohibition.

### Pre-authorized freedom

Within a customer-chosen safe range, the best protection may be *no interruption
at all*. A budget can protect intentions by making ordinary spending confidently
permissible instead of requiring repeated self-policing.

### Friction proportional to consequence

The customer may want a glance when comfortably within intention, a deliberate
review near a boundary, a cooling-off option for discretionary purchases, or an
explicit exception when something protected would change. One fixed amount of
friction is unlikely to fit every moment.

### A cooling-off path

`Wait until tomorrow`, `Revisit after payday`, or `Save this question` can
protect against urgency without declaring the purchase wrong. Cooling off must
be optional and customer-owned, never imposed as punishment.

### A scoped second person

Some people want a partner or trusted person involved only above a chosen amount
or when a shared commitment would change. The protection is a prepared decision
handoff, not surveillance, default permission-seeking, or full financial access.

### A one-time exception that stays one-time

Necessary or values-consistent exceptions should not silently weaken the
ongoing plan. A temporary exception can acknowledge reality, name its consequence,
and expire without shame or permanent policy drift.

### Protection from bad evidence

Sometimes the danger is not the customer's impulse but the app's stale total,
wrong category, missing transaction, or incomplete account coverage. Kwilt
should stop a consequential answer when its evidence cannot support one and
help correct truth before asking for a spending choice.

### Recovery without punishment

After an unplanned purchase, the useful question becomes `What now?` Protection
means finding the smallest recovery path while preserving dignity—not presenting
a failure state, lost streak, or escalating restriction.

## The question families beneath people's exact words

Customers may use unique language, but their questions often request one or
more bounded answer dimensions:

### 1. Permission or plan fit

- `Is this in the budget?`
- `Do I have room for this?`
- `Am I allowed to spend this?`
- `Is this okay?`

Needed answer: whether the proposed spend fits the customer's confirmed plan
and what remains afterward.

### 2. Timing or liquidity

- `Can I do this before payday?`
- `Will this account be okay until Social Security comes?`
- `Are the bills covered first?`
- `How long does this money need to last?`

Needed answer: supported cash-flow boundary, upcoming obligations, and evidence
gaps. A category limit alone cannot answer this.

### 3. Trade-off

- `What would I have to give up?`
- `Where would the money come from?`
- `Can I make this fit?`
- `Which thing gets tighter?`

Needed answer: the smallest complete consequence chain with protected items
named explicitly.

### 4. Intention or self-protection

- `Did I say I was going to stop doing this?`
- `What am I trying to protect?`
- `Should I wait?`
- `Will I regret this?`

Needed answer: return the customer's own prior rule, reason, cooling-off
preference, or chosen escalation. Kwilt cannot determine regret or moral worth.

### 5. Truth or anomaly

- `Why is this number so high?`
- `What is missing?`
- `Why is this in Groceries?`
- `Does this total include the refund?`

Needed answer: source records, counting semantics, freshness, uncertainty, and
the consequence of correction.

### 6. Plan adaptation

- `Is the budget wrong now?`
- `Was this month unusual?`
- `Should I change the category?`
- `Will this happen again?`

Needed answer: distinguish one-time evidence from durable change and propose,
but never silently apply, the relevant plan update.

### 7. Household authority

- `Can we spend this?`
- `Do I need to ask my partner?`
- `What did we agree?`
- `Can I answer this without seeing everything?`

Needed answer: scoped facts, the shared rule if one exists, and the person whose
authority is genuinely required.

## The questions should not follow one universal script

A fixed questionnaire would recreate the decision burden in a different form.
The preferred order is conditional:

### Step 1: Preserve the customer's actual first question

Do not immediately translate `Can I buy these tires?` into `Select a category`.
Retain the wording as the visible decision being resolved.

### Step 2: Use context before asking

Infer the amount, likely category, time boundary, active protection, and account
or household scope from the originating surface when trustworthy. Treat
inference as provisional, not customer intent.

### Step 3: Identify the answer dimensions required

`Is this in the budget?` requires plan fit. `Can I do this before payday?`
requires timing evidence. `What would I give up?` requires a consequence
scenario. Do not calculate or display dimensions the question does not need
unless they reveal a material risk.

### Step 4: Ask at most one materially branching clarification

Ask only when plausible answers lead to meaningfully different conclusions.
Examples:

- `Is the $240 for the tire itself or the full installed total?`
- `Should Kwilt check this against Auto or your emergency reserve?`
- `Are you asking whether it fits the monthly plan or whether checking can cover
  it before Friday?`

If every plausible interpretation produces the same result, answer without
interrupting.

### Step 5: Give the scoped answer first

Use one of the plain states:

- fits the rules you set;
- fits with one named trade-off;
- does not fit the confirmed rules;
- cannot tell yet, with the missing evidence named.

### Step 6: Return the relevant protection

Name the customer's chosen intention or boundary only when it applies:
`You chose to keep the repair reserve untouched` or `You asked Kwilt to pause
shopping when Clothing runs ahead of pace`.

### Step 7: Offer the fewest materially different next moves

Buy, wait, use eligible room, accept a temporary exception, revise the ongoing
plan, correct the evidence, ask the right household member, or leave. The user
should not need to choose among multiple versions of the same action.

### Step 8: Record authority, not imagined behavior

Record an opened access window, saved plan change, correction, exception, or
decision handoff. Do not claim the user purchased something merely because they
opened an app or explored a scenario.

## Delivery should follow the moment

The same Money-owned answer can arrive through different established or future
modalities without creating different financial truth:

| Moment shape | Best doorway | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Spending app is about to open | Existing Screen Time shield and category handoff | Kwilt can create a customer-chosen pause at the exact impulse boundary |
| Customer is already viewing Money | Summary, category detail, or adjustment flow | Context supplies category, month, plan, and evidence without new navigation |
| Customer has a question but not a destination | Unified Chat | Natural language preserves the person's mental model and routes to bounded Money answers |
| Physical purchase, bill, estimate, or cart | Later voice, share, or camera entry | Amount and merchant context can be captured without manual app navigation |
| Recurring personal ritual | Privacy-minimized widget, calm notification, or explicitly scheduled text | Answer can arrive at payday, bill day, or a chosen review moment |
| Another person's authority is needed | Scoped household decision handoff | Shares the decision and consequence rather than broad account access |
| Evidence is wrong | Exact transaction, account, or plan-correction destination | Correction belongs with authoritative source data, not a conversational approximation |

The delivery rule is: **bring the answer to the moment when possible; when an
OS or privacy boundary requires app entry, return to the exact Money object and
preserve the question that brought the customer there.**

## Current Kwilt delivery review

### What is already strong

- Money is not starting from a blank screen: Summary, Transactions, Accounts,
  category detail, forecasts, freshness, correction, governed plan previews,
  receipts, reserves, widgets, bounded Chat evidence, and Screen Time controls
  already cover substantial financial truth.
- Screen Time policies already express meaningful self-protection choices:
  always review, when hot, at 95%, when over, and when transactions need review.
- The handoff preserves the relevant category and offers a reversible, scoped
  access choice: open selected apps for 20 minutes or keep them blocked.
- Category detail distinguishes spent, left, limit, percentage used, month
  elapsed, funding rhythm, forecast, transaction activity, and freshness.
- Governed category changes preview whole-plan impact and protect fixed or
  user-owned amounts before a save.

### Where the interaction remains incomplete

- Summary currently leads with category tiles and `spent / planned`, then a
  total and remaining amount. It presents useful truth but does not identify the
  customer's hot question or synthesize the decision answer.
- A Screen Time trigger knows its category and reason, but the handoff currently
  asks `Open for 20 min` or `Keep blocked` before learning the contemplated
  amount, purchase, or specific concern.
- Category detail contains the evidence needed for judgment, but the customer
  must reconcile meter, pace, projection, limit, transaction history, and whole
  plan mentally.
- The current protection explains *that* a pause was configured, but does not
  consistently return *what the customer intended to protect*.
- Plan previews help during category editing; they do not yet begin from the
  spending decision that motivated the rebalance.
- Chat can access bounded Money evidence, but there is not yet a proven typed
  router from each question family to a scoped, authoritative decision answer.
- Physical-store, bill, estimate, payday, household-authority, and post-purchase
  recovery moments are less directly served than digital spending-app moments.

## Revised design challenge

**How might Kwilt recognize which financial decision is hot, return the
customer's chosen protection, and answer the exact question they are asking
with current Money evidence—asking at most one materially necessary
clarification—so the customer is left with only the values decision that truly
belongs to them?**

## Implication for convergence

The Spending Decision Answer remains promising, but a single generic purchase
simulator is too narrow. The next convergence should define a **critical-moment
answer system**:

- one Money-owned truth and scenario engine;
- several bounded question families;
- conditional evidence requirements;
- a maximum-one-clarification discipline;
- customer-chosen protection returned at the right moment;
- delivery through the established doorway closest to the trigger;
- explicit scope when Money can answer only plan fit, timing, or transaction
  truth rather than universal affordability.

The first learning release should choose one high-value moment and one adjacent
question family, not attempt every trigger or modality at once.
