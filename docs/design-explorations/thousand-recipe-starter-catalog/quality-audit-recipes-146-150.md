## Identity checks

- Piadina follows the protected thicker Romagna specification and its classic prosciutto, squacquerone, and arugula filling.
- Croque monsieur balances béchamel, jambon de Paris, Gruyère, and pain de mie while preserving crisp bread.
- Jambon-beurre stays rigorously focused on fresh baguette, cultured butter, and mild cooked ham, with cornichons alongside.
- Pan bagnat follows Nice’s official tomato-and-olive-oil-bathed raw-vegetable, tuna, egg, anchovy, olive, and basil structure with no mayonnaise.
- Welsh rarebit uses the St John roux, stout, mustard, Worcestershire, and mature Cheddar method rather than plain cheese on toast.

## Safety and execution checks

- All dairy, ham, egg, tuna, and anchovy ingredients are held for same-day assembly and immediate service.
- Pan bagnat eggs cook for 10 minutes and the pressed sandwich rests only 30 minutes at cool room temperature.
- Roux-based sauces cook until biscuit-scented before liquid or cheese is added.
- Broiler steps are short, close-watched, and happen only after the bread is already toasted.

## Highest-rated pattern synthesis

- These culturally protected and minimalist foods often lack meaningful public star pools; official specifications, city formulas, French reporting, and high-volume restaurant practice are the more probative evidence.
- Welsh rarebit’s consumer source is only 3.7/5 across 36 ratings, while the St John version has sold in sustained volume for decades. The authored version therefore uses the chef method and treats the rating as corroboration of the ingredient family, not proof of superiority.
- Croque sources converge on a smooth béchamel, thin cooked ham, Gruyère, and a final gratin, with crispness depending on sauce restraint and pre-toasting.
- For jambon-beurre, French chefs consistently attribute quality to bread, butter, ham, and made-to-order service rather than additions.

## Carry-forward rules

- Prefer an official protected formula when a regional food has one.
- Minimal recipes still require precise temperature, texture, proportion, and holding instructions.
- Do not use low or middling ratings as “best” evidence when stronger cultural and operational evidence explains the successful technique.
- When a dish’s name encodes moisture, oiling, or pressing, that physical treatment must be explicit in the method.
