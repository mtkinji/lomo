## Identity checks

- Chicken-fried steak uses thin beef, a rested double dredge, shallow frying, and peppered cream gravy; the name describes the frying style, not the protein.
- Red-eye gravy remains a thin coffee-and-ham-dripping pan gravy with no flour or dairy.
- Jambalaya selects the brown, tomato-free Cajun branch and explicitly respects tomato-rich New Orleans and Creole forms as distinct traditions.
- Shrimp étouffée uses shell stock, blond roux, trinity, and separately cooked rice; one tablespoon tomato paste marks a Creole lean without turning it into shrimp Creole.
- Blackened redfish uses true red drum and the historic butter-spice-hot-cast-iron technique, with the dish’s Cajun-chef and New Orleans restaurant context made transparent.

## Safety and execution checks

- Mechanically tenderized steak reaches 145°F and rests at least 3 minutes; raw country ham follows the same endpoint while fully cooked ham is heated according to its label.
- Jambalaya chicken reaches 165°F after the closed rice cook.
- Shrimp and redfish reach 145°F, with shrimp added only at the end.
- Blackening explicitly requires outdoor cooking or powerful ventilation and warns about smoke, hot fat, and flare risk.

## Highest-rated pattern synthesis

- No exact star-and-count pair was visible on the selected authoritative regional sources, so this set records no unsupported rating metadata.
- The strongest repeated signals are structural: coating adhesion and oil control for chicken-fried steak; minimal, flourless reduction for red-eye gravy; fond and closed-pan hydration for jambalaya; late shrimp addition for étouffée; and a thin spice coat in very hot cast iron for redfish.

## Carry-forward rules

- Cajun and Creole labels require recipe-level evidence; they are not interchangeable seasoning adjectives.
- When a dish has multiple legitimate regional branches, select one clearly and describe the others without ranking them.
- A technique name such as “blackened” must retain the technique that gives it meaning.
