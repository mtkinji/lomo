## Desirability and naming check

- Salmon en papillote is a familiar salmon-and-vegetable dinner with a low-cleanup packet method; its description immediately defines the technique.
- Steak frites, creamy Dijon chicken, Chicken Provençal, and pork tenderloin with apples and cider are recognizable meal names with familiar core ingredients.
- Chicken chasseur and blanquette de veau retain culturally meaningful names, but their descriptions identify them as mushroom-braised chicken and creamy veal stew.
- Poulet basquaise is titled `Poulet basquaise (Basque pepper chicken)` so its familiar chicken-and-pepper form is visible without opening the recipe.
- Lamb navarin is titled `Lamb navarin (French spring stew)` for the same reason.
- Duck confit is the only title in this group intentionally left as a culinary discovery term because its cure-and-fat-cook technique is the defining identity, not decorative naming.

## Complexity and recommendation check

- Creamy Dijon chicken is the clearest ordinary weeknight meal: 40 minutes, one skillet, supermarket ingredients, and no specialized equipment.
- Salmon en papillote takes 35 minutes and uses one sheet pan; parchment folding is its only unfamiliar action.
- Chicken Provençal, pork tenderloin with apples and cider, and steak frites fall into moderate cooking: 55 to 75 minutes, understandable ingredients, and explicit sequencing.
- Chicken chasseur and poulet basquaise are approachable one-pan braises of 80 to 90 minutes; they belong in relaxed dinner or weekend contexts, not quick filters.
- Blanquette de veau and lamb navarin are long but recognizable stew projects and must remain weekend recommendations.
- Duck confit is the sole high-burden project: a 12-hour cure, several hours of cooking, and substantial duck fat. It is restricted to discovery, weekend, and special-occasion contexts.
- No recipe in this group depends on luxury restaurant equipment. The two deep-frying or fat-cooking recipes include explicit temperature and handling controls.

## Identity checks

- Salmon en papillote uses a fully sealed parchment-steaming method with thin vegetables, wine, lemon, herbs, and butter.
- Duck confit preserves the salt cure, low cooking beneath duck fat, and separate final crisping.
- Steak frites treats twice-fried potatoes as half the dish and builds peppercorn sauce from steak fond.
- Chicken chasseur remains a white-wine, brandy, mushroom, tomato, and tarragon hunter-style braise.
- Blanquette de veau remains intentionally pale, with no meat or roux browning and a tempered cream-yolk liaison.
- Creamy Dijon chicken is a quick mustard-tarragon cutlet supper, distinct from the neighboring braises.
- Chicken Provençal is grounded in southern French tomato, garlic, olive, white-wine, herb, and citrus flavors.
- Pork tenderloin balances apples and cider with onion, stock, mustard, thyme, and vinegar rather than turning the pan sauce sweet.
- Poulet basquaise braises chicken in piperade with red and green peppers, tomato, onion, Espelette pepper, and cured ham.
- Lamb navarin uses lamb shoulder, light tomato-herb gravy, and staged spring vegetables rather than becoming a generic dark winter stew.

## Safety and execution checks

- Salmon reaches 145°F and packets are opened away from the face.
- Duck cures under refrigeration, cooks until collagen-tender, and reheats to 165°F; the recipe makes no shelf-stable preservation claim.
- Steak reaches 145°F with a three-minute rest; potatoes have explicit 325°F and 375°F frying stages; cognac is added off flame.
- All chicken breasts reach 165°F and all chicken thighs reach at least 175°F for safe, tender dark meat.
- Veal and lamb reach at least 145°F and continue until fork-tender.
- Pork tenderloin reaches 145°F and rests at least three minutes.
- Pasteurized egg yolks are used in blanquette and its liaison reaches 160°F without boiling.
- Every listed ingredient is allocated to preparation, cooking, finishing, or service.
