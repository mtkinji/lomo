## Familiarity and effort check

- Grilled steak, steamed fish, and bulgogi are familiar protein-centered meals
  using an ordinary skillet, grill, or covered pan. Each has under 30 minutes
  of active cooking.
- Bibimbap has several components but reuses one saucepan and one skillet. Its
  vegetable-by-vegetable sequencing is visible effort rather than hidden
  complexity, and the bowl remains a high-recognition household favorite.
- Dakgangjeong is the sole true project: starch coating, three first-fry
  batches, three hotter second-fry batches, a separately reduced glaze, and
  temperature-controlled oil. The recipe labels that burden honestly.
- Four recipes use ordinary equipment and readily obtainable primary
  ingredients. Toasted rice, fish sauce, gochujang, sesame oil, and Asian pear
  are stable pantry or produce items; apple is disclosed as the pear substitute.
- The batch has no discovery-tier recipes and no meal retained merely for
  geographic coverage.

## Safety and execution checks

- Crying-tiger steak reaches the USDA 145°F whole-cut target and rests before
  cross-grain slicing. Excess marinade is blotted before high-heat cooking.
- Steamed fish sits over cabbage rather than boiling in liquid and reaches
  145°F. The raw lime-garlic sauce is loosened with measured hot steaming
  liquid only after the fish is cooked.
- Bulgogi uses correctly identified Korean brewed soy sauce rather than salty
  soup soy sauce. Thin beef is marinated under refrigeration, cooked in two
  batches, and reaches at least 145°F.
- Bibimbap ground beef reaches 160°F. Sprouts and spinach are blanched,
  vegetables are cooked separately, and wet spinach is squeezed before service.
- Dakgangjeong chicken reaches 165°F during a 325°F first fry, then receives a
  short 375°F crisping fry. The pot remains no more than half full, oil returns
  to temperature between batches, and chicken drains on racks.
- Every listed ingredient is allocated to preparation, cooking, finishing, or
  service.
