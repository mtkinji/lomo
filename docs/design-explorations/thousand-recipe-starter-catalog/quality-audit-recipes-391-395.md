## Familiarity and effort check

- Red curry and panang are one-pan chicken dinners using commercial Thai curry
  paste, coconut milk, and jasmine rice. Both take about 40 to 50 minutes.
- Drunken noodles and pineapple fried rice are roughly 40-minute restaurant
  favorites. They use ordinary skillets or woks and small-batch sequencing to
  protect texture on a home stove.
- At least four recipes use ordinary equipment and ingredients obtainable from
  a well-stocked supermarket, with makrut lime leaves, holy basil, Thai curry
  pastes, and tamarind the only likely Asian-market items.
- Crispy fish is the sole frying-heavy meal. It uses skin-on fillets and 1/4
  inch of oil rather than forcing whole-fish deep frying into an ordinary
  dinner. It remains a cuisine anchor rather than a discovery project because
  it takes about 50 minutes and no specialist equipment.
- The batch has no discovery-tier recipes and no item retained merely for
  geographic coverage.

## Safety and execution checks

- Red-curry and panang chicken reach 165°F; both use a gentle coconut simmer,
  and panang remains thick without leaving chicken undercooked.
- Drunken-noodle chicken reaches 165°F. Its measured oil includes the extra
  teaspoon used to keep prepared dried noodles separate, and high-heat work is
  split into protein, vegetables, and two noodle batches.
- Pineapple-fried-rice shrimp reach 145°F and are removed before rice frying.
  Pineapple is drained and dried, eggs are barely set separately, and cold rice
  is broken apart before it reaches the pan.
- Fish is dried twice, lightly dusted, fried away from the cook in two batches
  at 350°F, and taken to 145°F. Hot oil is transferred only to a heatproof
  container, and the sauce is cooked after the fry.
- Every listed ingredient is allocated to preparation, cooking, finishing, or
  service.
