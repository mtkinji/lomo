# Diverge: Kwilt 2.0 Games maturity

Axis: breadth versus confidence versus depth.

## A. Polish everything equally

Keep all ten production-visible and add copy, motion, and sound throughout. This preserves breadth but treats surface polish as a substitute for a stronger loop. Best when every game is already fun; fails when prompt activities still lack payoff.

System fit is high, evidence quality is low. Anti-pattern check: fails the honesty bar unless every table is playtested.

## B. Earned catalog

Define one release bar, keep games with complete loops in the 2.0 shelf, move lower-confidence games into a development Workshop, and make local launch work without forced naming. The catalog remains structurally complete, but production promises only what has earned billing.

System fit is high: it reuses the catalog and Slanguage's gating pattern. Best when the week is short and brand confidence matters; fails if “not visible in production” is mistaken for retiring the work.

## C. One flagship game

Reduce Games to Bank and spend the week on a singular signature experience. This offers maximum depth and the clearest QA target, but throws away several already-coherent local loops and weakens the “easy shared play” promise.

System fit is medium and product breadth falls sharply. Best for a standalone game launch; fails Kwilt 2.0's existing-capability maturity goal.

## D. Playtest operating system

Keep the full catalog behind an internal gate and build detailed per-round analytics, surveys, and experiment controls. It creates better evidence later but adds instrumentation before improving the immediate start experience.

System fit is medium and the blast radius is high. It risks turning family play into a measured funnel.

All surviving options leave Arcs, Goals, Activities, and Chapters untouched, do not block capture, and avoid dashboards, streaks, public sharing, or anthropomorphic AI.

## Clue Circle loop alternatives

Axis: creative constraint versus rapid throughput.

### Keep the changing-clue puzzle

Retain three targets per finder and let a backward gesture change how the audience may clue the same target. This is the most distinctive version, but it makes the room stop and reinterpret instructions during play and gives a difficult target no natural ending.

### Timed rapid circle

Give each finder a short timed turn. A practice downward tilt starts play and confirms the motion with sound; the same gesture marks correct, an upward tilt passes, and both immediately reveal the next target. Scores contribute to one circle total after everyone plays. This preserves the physical prop and Kwilt's cooperative tone while restoring urgency and flow.

### Untimed family-memory circle

Remove scoring and let each target become a family-story prompt. This may create warm conversation, but it no longer promises the kinetic guessing game Andrew is asking to mature and overlaps other connection tables.

The timed rapid circle best answers the agreed experience hypothesis: the finder becomes the center of a burst of group collaboration, and each correct answer creates a physical shared celebration.
